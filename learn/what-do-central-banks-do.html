What do central banks do?

Central banks manage a nation’s money and credit. Most people never interact with one directly — yet their decisions ripple through mortgages, savings, share prices, and currencies all over the world.

4 min read

By Thomas Amara · 22 July 2026

The Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England — these are central banks, and they sit at the centre of the financial system. Understanding what they’re actually trying to do explains an enormous amount about why markets move.

The core job: price stability

A central bank’s main task is usually price stability — keeping inflation low and steady. Many have a broader remit too: the US Federal Reserve has a "dual mandate" of stable prices and maximum employment, and most also work to safeguard financial stability. Crucially, they’re typically kept independent of day-to-day politics, so policy isn’t bent to fit the electoral cycle.

The main lever: interest rates

The headline tool is the policy interest rate — the rate that influences borrowing costs across the whole economy. It works in two directions:

  • Raising rates makes borrowing more expensive, cooling demand and bringing inflation down.
  • Cutting rates makes borrowing cheaper, stimulating spending, investment, and growth.

This single lever is the one markets watch most closely of all, because it feeds into everything from mortgage costs to the yield curve to how companies are valued.

Lender of last resort

In a crisis, a central bank acts as the lender of last resort. If a fundamentally sound bank suddenly can’t access cash, the central bank can step in and provide emergency funding — stopping a panic or a bank run from cascading through the rest of the system. This role is a big part of why the modern financial system holds together during shocks.

The other functions

Beyond rates and crises, central banks also issue the physical currency, oversee the payment systems that move money around, and in many countries regulate and supervise the banks themselves. They manage the nation’s foreign-exchange reserves, and some intervene in currency markets.

Why independence matters

Operational independence lets a central bank make unpopular decisions — like raising rates into a slowdown — for the sake of long-term stability. There’s a subtle payoff here: a credible, independent central bank does a better job of anchoring people’s expectations of future inflation. And when everyone expects inflation to stay near target, controlling it actually becomes easier. Credibility, in other words, is a tool in its own right.

Key takeaways

  • A central bank’s core job is price stability — low, steady inflation.
  • Its main lever is the policy interest rate: raise to cool, cut to stimulate.
  • In a crisis it acts as lender of last resort to solvent banks.
  • Independence lets it make unpopular calls and anchor inflation expectations.
