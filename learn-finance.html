Can you learn finance on your own?

Yes. Finance is built from a small number of ideas, such as ownership, lending, risk, the time value of money and the role of central banks, and each one can be learned independently in short daily sessions, provided they are learned in the right order.

The word finance covers three overlapping subjects, and it helps to know which one you are looking for. Personal finance concerns budgeting, saving, pensions and managing debt. Corporate finance concerns how companies raise money, report their results, value themselves and decide where to invest. Markets and investing concerns how stocks, bonds, currencies and commodities are priced and how portfolios are built. This roadmap focuses on corporate finance and markets, which are the foundation for investing well and for most careers in the industry, though much of it will also sharpen your personal financial decisions.

Most people who give up on finance do so not because the material is too difficult but because they begin with the most complicated topics, such as options or trading strategies, before they understand what a share or a bond actually represents. When the concepts are taken in sequence, each one makes the next easier.

How long does it take to learn finance?

With around 15 minutes a day, most beginners can follow financial news within a month, reach a working understanding of markets and valuation within three months, and be ready for technical interviews within six to twelve months.

MilestoneTypical time at 15 min/dayWhat you can do
Financial literacy4 weeksFollow market news and understand what stocks, bonds and interest rates are doing
Working fluency3 monthsRead company results, compare valuations and explain the economic cycle
Confident investor6 monthsBuild and judge a diversified portfolio and understand the risks you are taking
Interview-ready6–12 monthsAnswer technical questions on statements, DCF, LBOs and M&A, and discuss recent market events

These are typical ranges for consistent daily study rather than guarantees; prior study of economics or accounting will shorten them.

Consistency matters far more than intensity. Spaced, repeated exposure is how concepts move into long-term memory, which is why fifteen minutes every day produces better results than a long session once a week.

Whichever format you choose, three habits make the difference: learning concepts in sequence, testing yourself immediately after each one, and applying the idea to something real, such as the day's market news or a company's latest results.

How to learn investing by doing

Investing is a skill of judgement under uncertainty, and judgement improves through decisions and feedback rather than reading alone. The difficulty is that real investing gives slow and noisy feedback, and mistakes cost real money.

A useful alternative is to practise on market history. Take a real episode, such as the 2008 collapse of Lehman Brothers, the UK gilt crisis of September 2022 or the failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023; decide what you would have done with only the information available at the time; and then compare your call with what actually happened. Doing this repeatedly builds the pattern recognition that experienced investors rely upon.

Two lessons emerge quickly from this kind of practice. First, position sizing matters as much as being right, because a correct call made with a small position can be worth less than a modest error made with a large one. Second, survival comes before returns, since a portfolio that suffers a large loss needs a much larger gain to recover. Our guides to risk and return and diversification explain the principles behind both.

Learning finance for a career

If you are aiming for investment banking, private equity, asset management or equity research, the bar is higher and more specific. Interviewers expect you to explain how the three financial statements link, walk through a DCF, describe how an LBO generates returns, build an enterprise value bridge, and discuss a recent deal or market event with genuine understanding of the mechanism behind it.

The most effective preparation combines the full roadmap above with repeated drilling of technical questions in the months before applications open, and daily attention to markets so that commercial awareness becomes second nature rather than something revised the night before an interview.

Frequently asked questions

Can I learn finance for free?
Yes. The core concepts of finance, from stocks and bonds to valuation, macroeconomics and portfolio theory, can be learned entirely for free through structured guides, free university courses and apps such as Basis, whose 11 learning tracks and daily market game are free.
How long does it take to learn finance?
With around 15 minutes a day, most beginners can follow financial news within four weeks, reach a working understanding of markets, statements and valuation within three months, and be ready for technical finance interviews within six to twelve months of consistent study.
Do I need to be good at maths to learn finance?
No. Most of finance relies on arithmetic, percentages and a few ideas such as compounding and discounting. More advanced areas, such as derivatives pricing or quantitative finance, use more mathematics, but they are not needed to understand markets, read financial statements or invest sensibly.
What is the best way to learn finance as a beginner?
Learn the foundations in a sensible order, starting with what stocks and bonds are, risk and return, and inflation, then move to financial statements, valuation and macroeconomics. Study a little every day, test yourself after each concept, and apply what you learn to real market events.
Can you learn finance with an app?
Yes, provided the app teaches each concept before it tests it and follows a structured curriculum. Short daily lessons suit how memory works, and apps that add practice on real market events help turn definitions into judgement.
How do I learn finance for investment banking interviews?
Master the three financial statements and how they link, the main valuation methods (comparable companies, precedent transactions and the DCF), LBO and M&A mechanics, and enterprise value. Then drill technical questions repeatedly and follow markets closely enough to discuss a recent deal or market event.
What is the difference between finance and accounting?
Accounting records and reports what a business has done, through the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement. Finance uses that information to make decisions about the future, such as how to value a company, how to fund it and where to invest.
